Review of Key Terms

Tip

You may wish to reference this Tidy R Cheatsheet when completing this lab.

Tidy data

A rectangular data table in which every row is an observation and every column is a variable describing something about that observation

Pivoting

Rotating data columns so that they are presented as rows or rotating data rows so that they are presented as columns

HUD’s Point-in Time Counts

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development is responsible for monitoring and addressing housing affordability and homelessness throughout the country. One initiative that they oversee towards this end is the Continuum of Care Program. Continuums of Care (CoCs) are local planning organizations responsible for allocating resources and coordinating services to address homelessness in the United States. Every state has a number of CoCs that report to the Department of Housing and Urban Development.

Every year, on a single night in the last 10 days of January, CoCs required to conduct a “point-in-time” count of sheltered and unsheltered homeless individuals. To generate the sheltered point-in-time count, CoCs coordinate with transitional housing centers or emergency shelters to record the number of people housed in that location on the selected night. There are a few different approaches to generating an unsheltered point-in-time count:

  1. “Night of” street count: Volunteers are sent out to canvass either the entire geography of a random sample of areas in a CoC. While canvassing, they are expected to record the number of people they see currently residing in spaces not designed for sleeping accommodations.

  2. Service-based count: In the 7 days following the night of the designated PIT count, volunteers are dispatched to food kitchens, shelters, libraries and other services identified as spaces that unhoused individuals frequent. There, they are expected to survey individuals to determine if they were sheltered on the night of the count.

Following the night of the count, the collected data points are shipped off to HUD where they are aggregated into the dataset we will be working with today.

There are a number of limitations to this approach of estimating homelessness, which I encourage you to consider throughout this analysis and when responding to our Ethical Considerations question.

The next issue is that there are multiple kinds of demographics in the demographic column. Specifically, we have both race represented in that column and gender represented in that column. Remember the rules of tidy data, according to Wickham (2014):

  1. Each variable forms a column.
  2. Each observation forms a row.
  3. Each type of observational unit forms a table.

This issue violates the third rule. In one table, we have two observational units - a count of unhoused individuals by race and a count of unhoused individuals by gender. To clean this up, we need to separate this into different tables.

Ethical Considerations

Over the past several years, the Department of Housing and Urban Development has been updating how sex/gender gets classified when it comes to collecting data about the unhoused population. Recognizing the particular housing vulnerabilities of folks that do not identify as male or female, HUD felt it important to expand the way that gender was categorized in the data. In 2022, available options for recording gender in the dataset you analyzed today included “female,” “male,” “transgender,” “gender-questioning,” and “not singularly female or male.” While this expanded the way that gender could be recorded in this dataset, the US census has not similarly expanded their gender categories. In the US census, people are listed as either Male or Female. This poses a challenge. Without accounting for the overall population of each of these genders when analyzing this data, the total number of unhoused individuals that do not identify as male or female may appear very small when in fact the proportion of unhoused individuals that do not identify as male or female may be quite high. We can’t see this proportion without overall population data - data that would be very difficult to collect. On the other hand, if we were to try and map the more current data onto the existing census categories, we would literally clean folks that identify as “transgender,” “gender-questioning,” and “not singularly female or male” out of being represented in the data. With all of this in mind, what do you think is the most ethical or just way to display the most recent point in time count data? What would need to happen to make our data about the unhoused more representative? What are the some of the benefits to different communities to having their identities represented in this data?
